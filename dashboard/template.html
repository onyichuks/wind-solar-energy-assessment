Penmanshiel wind farm: predicted against actual

A pre-construction style energy yield assessment for a 14-turbine, 28.7 MW wind farm in the Scottish Borders, then a test of that estimate against 58 months of metered production, with the gap traced cause by cause. A machine learning model of each turbine then flags underperformance. A separate solar module reviews a small PV system on a different site: NREL in Golden, Colorado.

Objective

Pre-construction energy yield assessments decide whether wind farms are financed, but they are rarely checked against what the farm later produces. Penmanshiel is one of very few wind farms with a complete public operating record, so every major assumption can be measured after the fact. The project set out to answer five questions:

  1. The estimate. What would a pre-construction style assessment have predicted, using only information available before construction?
  2. The test. How did actual production compare, and was the difference within the stated uncertainty?
  3. The explanation. Which assumptions explain the difference, measured from turbine SCADA?
  4. Machine learning. Can a normal-behaviour model predict each turbine's power better than a power curve, and does it pick out underperformance that the operator's records confirm?
  5. Solar (a separate site). How well would an independent, pre-construction style estimate have predicted a monitored PV system's energy (NREL, Golden, Colorado), and where was energy lost?

Conclusions

    The full write-up, with abstract, methods, results tables, discussion, limitations and references, is in the research report.

    How it was done

    Data checks. Downloads are verified against the published checksums. Every dataset is checked for gaps, duplicates, clock problems, out-of-range and frozen readings, and the meters are checked against each other and against the summed turbine meters. This found a stuck wind vane, one corrupt day of revenue meter data (replaced from the turbine meters), a counter gap in the controller meter, and a 14-day whole-farm outage in autumn 2020. The checks are repeatable with scripts/check_data.py and described in the dataset guide.

    Virtual met mast. The open data have no met mast, so one was built from the turbines: for each 10-minute interval the nacelle anemometers of turbines with no neighbour upwind (sectors from IEC 61400-12-1 Annex A) are averaged. Calendar 2017 stands in for the 12-month on-site measurement campaign a developer would run before construction.

    Long-term correction. The 2017 site wind is related to 25 years (2000 to 2024) of ERA5 reanalysis by direction sector. The measured distribution is scaled sector by sector to the long-term mean and reweighted to long-term sector frequencies. This kept the measured distribution shape and, in cross-validation between the two measured years, came within about 2 percent in energy, where hourly regression methods over-predicted by 2 to 9 percent. A machine learning method (gradient-boosted trees on ERA5 wind, direction, shear, temperature, time of day and season) was as accurate, within about 1 percent, and gives almost the same P50; it is offered in the long-term method selector.

    Energy and wakes. The Senvion MM82 reference power curve is adjusted to the long-term site air density (ERA5, moved to hub height). DTU's PyWake runs four engineering wake models on the real layout over a 5 degree by 0.5 m/s frequency table. The central estimate uses the Park model with the classic onshore wake decay constant (k = 0.075); the default Bastankhah Gaussian, TurbOPark and Niayifar models are sensitivities.

    Losses and uncertainty. Availability, electrical, turbine performance and environmental losses use typical pre-construction assumptions. Uncertainties for measurement, long-term correction, flow, power curve, wakes and losses are combined by root sum of squares, with wind speed terms converted to energy by the modelled sensitivity ratio. Interannual variability is added for 1, 10 and 20-year P values.

    Predicted against actual. The estimate is turned into a month-by-month prediction with an energy index: ERA5 wind each hour through the same wake-modelled farm power table, scaled so its long-term mean equals the P50. The monthly index uses an hourly sector regression; its level bias cancels because each month is divided by the index's own 25-year mean. Revenue meter energy (gaps filled from the wind farm controller meter) is compared for every complete month from September 2016 to June 2021, with an OpenOA-style regression as a cross-check.

    Explaining the gap. Turbine SCADA for 2017 and 2018 measures most assumptions over the same two years as the actual end point: availability from the operator's lost production records (checked with an independent calculation), electrical losses from turbine meters against the revenue meter, the power curve from the fleet's measured curve (IEC method of bins), terrain speed-up from free-stream nacelle wind at each position, and wake losses from production against terrain-adjusted expected power. Curtailment comes from the operator's turbine records for 2017 and 2018 (none), with the wind farm power setpoint as a check from December 2018. Each measured value replaces its assumption in turn; what is left is the residual.

    Turbine health (machine learning). One gradient-boosted tree model per turbine (scikit-learn) learns expected power from nacelle wind speed, turbulence, wind direction, and ERA5 air temperature and air density. Gradient boosting was chosen over random forest, extra trees, a neural network, k-nearest neighbours and spline regression by cross-validation within 2017. It is trained on 2017 normal operation, after a robust filter drops points far from each turbine's typical power, and tested on 2018 against each turbine's own power curve built from the same data. Daily actual energy is compared with expected energy; days more than a set percentage short are flagged, and a day when most turbines fall short together is treated as farm-wide. Flags are checked against the operator's performance-loss records and the turbine status logs.

    Solar. NREL PVDAQ system 50 (fixed tilt 45 degrees, azimuth 158 degrees). Daylight saving clock shifts are found with PVAnalytics (production midpoint against modelled solar noon) and corrected; values are moved back one 15-minute step, which gave the best clear-sky fit. Expected power uses pvlib: Erbs decomposition of satellite irradiance, Perez transposition, incidence angle losses, SAPM cell temperature and PVWatts. DC capacity is fitted on clear-sky periods. Outages, suspected snow cover and clipping are separated from the remaining performance gap. An independent estimate, made without the measured output, uses NREL's rated size, the generic PVWatts losses, 0.5% a year ageing and the satellite irradiance corrected month by month to 25 years of ERA5; it is then tested against 2012 and 2013 production, and the satellite irradiance is checked against NREL's on-site sensor.

    Limits. Nacelle anemometers are not calibrated met mast instruments, and their turbulence reads higher than ambient. Measured wake losses are a scaled estimate that depends on the terrain adjustment. Turbine SCADA covers 2017 and 2018 only, so 2019 to 2021 is compared but only partly explained: the meters show the 2020 outages, nothing more. The turbine health models learn 2017 as normal, so a fault present all through 2017 would not be flagged. The solar system is a small research array with satellite irradiance, so absolute performance ratio values depend on the fitted capacity.
